Galaxies and the Structure of the Universe

A galaxy is a massive collection of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter, all held together by gravity. Galaxies range dramatically in size, from small dwarf galaxies containing a few hundred million stars to giant galaxies containing over a trillion stars. Scientists estimate there are over two trillion galaxies in the observable universe.

Our solar system is located within the Milky Way galaxy, a barred spiral galaxy containing an estimated 100 to 400 billion stars. The Milky Way is about 100,000 light-years in diameter, and our solar system sits in one of its spiral arms, roughly 27,000 light-years from the galactic center. At the center of the Milky Way lies a supermassive black hole called Sagittarius A*, with a mass about 4 million times that of the Sun.

Galaxies are generally classified into three main shapes: spiral galaxies (like the Milky Way), which have a flat, rotating disk with spiral arms; elliptical galaxies, which are rounder and contain mostly older stars with little new star formation; and irregular galaxies, which have no defined shape, often as a result of gravitational interactions or collisions with other galaxies.

The Andromeda Galaxy is the closest large spiral galaxy to the Milky Way, located about 2.5 million light-years away. It is on a slow collision course with the Milky Way, and the two galaxies are expected to merge in about 4.5 billion years, forming a single, larger galaxy.

The universe itself is estimated to be about 13.8 billion years old, based on measurements of the cosmic microwave background radiation, a faint glow of radiation left over from the Big Bang. The Big Bang theory describes how the universe began as an extremely hot, dense point and has been expanding and cooling ever since. Evidence for this expansion comes from observations showing that distant galaxies are moving away from us, and the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it appears to be receding, a relationship known as Hubble's Law.

Dark matter and dark energy make up the vast majority of the universe's total mass and energy, roughly 95%, even though they cannot be directly observed. Dark matter is inferred from its gravitational effects on visible matter, such as the way galaxies rotate faster than visible matter alone can explain. Dark energy is thought to be responsible for the accelerating expansion of the universe, a discovery that earned the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics. Despite decades of research, the exact nature of both dark matter and dark energy remains one of the biggest unsolved mysteries in modern physics and cosmology.
