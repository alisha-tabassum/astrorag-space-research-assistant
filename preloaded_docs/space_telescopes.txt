Space Telescopes: Hubble and James Webb

The Hubble Space Telescope was launched by NASA in 1990 and has been orbiting Earth ever since, observing the universe without the distortion caused by Earth's atmosphere. Hubble orbits at an altitude of about 540 kilometers and completes an orbit roughly every 95 minutes. It has captured some of the most famous images in astronomy, including the "Pillars of Creation" in the Eagle Nebula and the "Hubble Deep Field," which revealed thousands of previously unseen galaxies in a tiny patch of sky. Hubble was serviced by astronauts on five separate Space Shuttle missions, allowing it to be repaired and upgraded over the decades, dramatically extending its working life far beyond its original planned mission length.

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), launched in December 2021, is considered the successor to Hubble, though it observes primarily in infrared light rather than visible light. This allows JWST to see through clouds of cosmic dust and observe some of the earliest galaxies formed after the Big Bang. Unlike Hubble, which orbits Earth, JWST orbits the Sun at a location called the second Lagrange point (L2), about 1.5 million kilometers from Earth, where it is shielded from the Sun's heat by a massive five-layer sunshield roughly the size of a tennis court. JWST's primary mirror is made of 18 hexagonal gold-coated beryllium segments and has a diameter of 6.5 meters, more than twice the size of Hubble's mirror, allowing it to collect significantly more light and see fainter, more distant objects.

Because JWST is so far from Earth, it cannot be serviced by astronauts the way Hubble was. Every component had to work correctly after a complex, multi-week unfolding sequence following launch, since any failure in that process could not be fixed afterward. The mission is considered one of the most technically ambitious in the history of space exploration.

Together, Hubble and JWST represent two generations of space-based astronomy. Hubble revolutionized our visual understanding of the cosmos and remains operational, continuing to contribute data alongside JWST. JWST is pushing the boundaries further, studying the atmospheres of exoplanets, the formation of stars and planetary systems, and galaxies that formed over 13 billion years ago, closer to the beginning of the universe than any telescope has observed before.
